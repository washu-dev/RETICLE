The co-hit channel

A second, independent way to connect two genes — not by how alike their scores look (channel 1), but by how often the original screen authors called both of them significant in the same experiment, more than chance predicts. Built and stress-tested today; three real problems are still open (§7) and are stated plainly, not glossed over.

Channel 1 (Pearson correlation on PERCENTILE_SCORE) is strong on constitutive machines — ribosome, proteasome, spliceosome — and weak on conditional genes, whose phenotype only shows up in a minority of screens. FANCA–FANCB scores just r = 0.41 there, diluted by ~950 screens where neither gene does anything. This channel asks a narrower question instead — among the screens where a gene mattered enough to be called a hit, does it keep showing up alongside the same partner? — using Fisher's exact test. On the same pair it answers with overwhelming confidence: 33 co-hits against an expectation of 3.6.

1 · Why a second channel

Channel 1 treats every screen the same way, hit or not: a gene's full numeric profile — deleterious, neutral, or advantageous — goes into a Pearson correlation. That works beautifully for genes that are always doing something (a ribosomal protein is essential in almost every screen), but it actively hurts genes that are only sometimes doing something. Fanconi-anemia genes are a clean example: FANCA and FANCB only really diverge from "nothing happening" in a small subset of screens — usually ones with a DNA-damaging or replication-stress component. In the other ~95% of screens, both genes sit near the noisy middle, and that shared silence drags the correlation toward zero.

The idea this channel tests. If a gene was never called a IS_HIT in a screen, that screen has nothing to say about it — treat the cell as missing, not as a weak zero. Keep only the screens where a gene actually did something, and ask whether two genes are significant together far more than their individual hit rates would predict on their own.

2 · The question, as an urn

Take the 985 genome-wide screens where both FANCA and FANCB were measured. Imagine them as 985 balls in an urn. FANCA was a hit in 94 of them — paint those 94 balls red. Now look at the 38 screens where FANCB was a hit, with no reference to FANCA at all. If FANCB's hits were scattered through the urn at random, how many of its 38 balls would you expect to be red, just by chance?

\[ \mathbb{E}[\text{co-hit}] \;=\; \frac{r_i \cdot r_j}{n} \;=\; \frac{94 \times 38}{985} \;=\; 3.626 \]
n = co-measured screens (985), r_i = gene i's hits within that population (94), r_j = gene j's hits within that population (38). This is just "38 draws from an urn that's 9.5% red, on average land ≈3.6 red."

The observed overlap is 9.1 times the chance expectation. Fisher's exact test turns "9.1× more than expected" into a calibrated probability: if FANCA and FANCB were truly independent, how likely is it that pure luck would hand you 33-or-more red balls in 38 draws? That's exactly what the hypergeometric distribution computes.

3 · The 2×2 table and the formula

Every gene pair reduces to four numbers, which fill in a standard contingency table:

\[ p \;=\; P(X \ge a) \;=\; \sum_{k=a}^{\min(r_i,r_j)} \frac{\binom{r_i}{k}\binom{n-r_i}{r_j-k}}{\binom{n}{r_j}} \]
The one-sided (enrichment) hypergeometric survival probability: draw r_j balls without replacement from an urn of n balls with r_i marked, and ask how likely a-or-more marked balls is. In code this is one call: hypergeom.sf(a-1, n, r_i, r_j) — the a-1 is because sf(k) gives P(X>k), not P(X≥k).

2.2×10⁻³¹ is not a "these might be related" number — it is a "this could not plausibly be chance" number. For comparison, a fair coin landing heads 20 times running has probability ≈9.5×10⁻⁷ — roughly 24 orders of magnitude more likely than this co-hit pattern arising by chance.

4 · Why not just correlate the hit-only screens?

The obvious alternative — keep PERCENTILE_SCORE and Pearson-correlate only the co-hit screens — was tried first and fails three separate ways:

Failure modeWhat happensReal example
Range restrictionConditioning on "is a hit" selects a gene's extreme tail — the variance Pearson needs is gonePSMB5 keeps 8% of its spread inside its own hit set; r(PSMB5,PSMB4) collapses 0.667 → 0.046
n=2 is arithmeticTwo points always fit a line exactly — every 2-co-hit pair scores |r|=1.000, the mathematical ceiling12% of all gene pairs in a 3,000-gene sample have exactly 2 co-hits — enough noise at r=1.0 to bury every real edge
No natural thresholdA hand-picked support floor is needed, and the whole "how much evidence" question is left unanswered by r itselfFisher's p-value already encodes evidence volume — nothing to hand-tune

Fisher fixes all three by construction: it needs no variance (only counts), degrades gracefully at n=2 (2 co-hits against expectation 0.4 gives p≈0.05 — correctly "not significant," not a fake perfect correlation), and evidence volume is already inside the p-value.

5 · Computing 224 million pairs without a loop

~21,000 candidate genes means ~224 million pairs. Calling scipy.stats.fisher_exact that many times would take days. The fix: all four ingredients of the 2×2 table are just matrix products over two 0/1 matrices — M (measured) and H (hit, zeroed wherever not measured) — each gene×screen.

\[ a[i,j] \;=\; (H \, H^{\top})[i,j] \;=\; \sum_{s} H[i,s]\cdot H[j,s] \]
Each screen s contributes 1 to the sum only when both H[i,s] and H[j,s] are 1 — i.e. only when both genes are hits in that screen. Summed over all screens, that is exactly the co-hit count. The other three matrices follow the identical logic with M in place of H on one side.
A real bug this caught. Matrix multiplication is floating point — counts that should be exact integers (like 985.0000000001) drift off-integer, and hypergeom silently returns NaN on non-integer inputs rather than erroring. Every matrix is rounded with np.rint() before being handed to hypergeom.sf — found via two real NaNs during validation, not by inspection.

7 · What's still open unresolved

This channel has not been through the same scrutiny channel 1 has (CORUM validation, shuffle-null threshold search). Three concrete problems are known and unfixed:

7a · The ranking key is wrong

Problem. Each gene's top-25 partners are currently chosen by smallest p-value, not by effect size. p-value conflates "how strong is this relationship" with "how much data do we have" — a pan-essential pair with huge marginals can out-rank a real, tight, low-data relationship by hundreds of orders of magnitude.
pairco-hitfold (effect size)p-value (current sort key)
RPL13–RPL3 (ribosome, generic)8351.5×1.53×10⁻²⁶⁴ ← ranks first
FANCA–FANCB (Fanconi, specific)339.1×2.21×10⁻³¹ ← ranks behind it

By the current sort key, a weak (1.5×) but data-heavy pair outranks a strong (9.1×), genuinely specific one. not fixed — the sort key should be fold (or a shrunk/regularized version of it, since raw fold is itself biased toward rare-hit genes), with q_value used only as a pass/fail gate.

7b · IS_HIT was never harmonized

Problem. Channel 1 exists specifically because raw scores from different labs (MaGeCK, CasTLE, STARS, Log2FC) aren't comparable — that's what PERCENTILE_SCORE fixes. This channel's entire signal is IS_HIT, which is the original screen authors' own significance call, inherited as-is. It was never put through that same harmonization.

Fisher's model implicitly assumes every screen calls hits at a comparable standard. It doesn't: a rare gene that happens to be a hit only in the "loosely-called" screens will look artificially co-hit with anything else called a hit in those same loose screens, purely from that shared laxity — not biology. not fixed — candidate repair is to re-derive a per-screen hit flag from a fixed PERCENTILE_SCORE quantile (so every screen's "hit" means the same relative extremeness), rather than trusting each screen's own threshold.

7c · Two-class artifact filter was hand-tuned, not derived

Problem. Spot-checking found two failure classes: genes with no real phenotype that still cluster (olfactory receptors, defensins — near-zero co-hits, mostly self-resolving) and multi-mapping sgRNA genes whose guides cut several loci at once, producing a real but non-biological "cutting toxicity" phenotype that mimics genuine co-essentiality (paralog/tandem-repeat families).
What was found to work, on spot-checks only. co_hit ≥ 20 AND concordance ≥ 0.90 AND fold ≥ 2.0 silences both known artifact examples (OR51A2, USP17L28) while keeping the full FANCD2 Fanconi neighborhood intact.

These three numbers were chosen by eyeballing a handful of known genes — exactly the "picking a cutoff by eye is arbitrary" problem channel 1's shuffle-null threshold (§7 of the Pearson doc) was built to avoid. not fixed — this channel needs the equivalent: a within-screen shuffle null, calibrated FDR, not a hand-picked triple.

Glossary

Fisher's exact testA statistical test for whether two binary outcomes (here: "hit in screen X") co-occur more than chance predicts, exact for any sample size (no large-sample approximation needed).
Hypergeometric distributionThe probability distribution of "marked balls drawn without replacement from an urn" — the engine under Fisher's exact test.
Co-hitA screen where both genes in a pair were independently called a hit by the original authors.
Fold (enrichment)Observed co-hits ÷ co-hits expected from the two genes' individual hit rates alone. The effect-size number; not currently used for ranking (§7a).
ConcordanceShare of co-hit screens where both genes moved the same direction (both deleterious or both advantageous). Real complexes fail together, not oppositely.
BH-FDR / q-valueBenjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate correction, applied across all pairs tested, not just the ones kept.
SupportCo-measured screens — the Fisher population size, n in the formula.
