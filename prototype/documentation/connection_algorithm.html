How RETICLE decides
two genes are connected

End to end: how we decide whether to draw a line between two genes using CRISPR screen data alone — consulting no literature, no GO, no STRING, no database. This page assumes no prior knowledge and builds up one step at a time.

🔌 One analogy to hold on to. Every CRISPR screen is a stress test on cells: switch off each gene in turn and see how badly the cell is affected. If switching a gene off kills the cell, that gene "mattered" in this test. If two genes are always important together, and unimportant together, test after test, they are probably on the same circuit — doing the same job. Those same-circuit genes are what we are looking for. The one rule: only compare tests of the same kind.
Draw a line between two genes if and only if, across a set of screens sharing one biological context, the effects of switching each of them off rise and fall in step. That agreement is measured straight from the data, with no external knowledge involved.
STEP 1

The raw material: one number per gene, per screen

The whole algorithm eats one table — harmonized_scores, 28.2 million rows. It compresses every "this gene was switched off in that screen" into a single number, the percentile score, running from −1 to +1:

Why a rank rather than the raw value? Because labs score with wildly different methods (MaGeCK, CasTLE, Log2FC, ...) whose magnitudes are simply not comparable. But "in the most extreme 5%" means the same thing in any screen. This step turns a mess of raw scores into one shared ruler. That table is our single source of truth — every later step is derived from it.

STEP 2

A gene's "fingerprint": its whole row across many screens

Picture the table as a grid: rows = genes, columns = screens, cells = that percentile number. Each gene is then one whole row — how it behaved in each of hundreds or thousands of screens. That row is the gene's behavioural fingerprint.

STEP 3 · CORE

A connection = two fingerprints that look alike

This is the heart of the algorithm, and it is plain: lay the two fingerprints on top of each other and see whether they rise and fall together. If they do, draw a line; if not, do not. The tool that measures "alike" is the correlation coefficient, written r, from −1 to +1 — the closer to +1, the more alike.

A small example you can actually work out

Suppose that across 5 screens in the "DNA damage" class, three genes have these percentiles (more negative = more essential):

genescr 1scr 2scr 3scr 4scr 5
FANCA−0.9−0.3−0.8−0.2−0.7
FANCB−0.8−0.4−0.9−0.1−0.6
TUBB−0.7−0.8−0.2−0.3−0.9

Look at FANCA and FANCB: both drop hard in screens 1, 3 and 5, and both lift in screens 2 and 4 — perfectly in step. What about TUBB? It is also frequently essential (the cytoskeleton is indispensable), but it rises and falls to its own rhythm, which does not line up with FANCA.

→ FANCA — FANCB: r ≈ +0.94 ✅ connect    FANCA — TUBB: r ≈ +0.10 ✗ do not connect

Hidden in that example is the key intuition: correlation first subtracts each gene's own average (this is called centring), and then asks — "when FANCA is more essential than its own norm, is FANCB also more essential than its own norm?" Which means:

TUBB may be "essential all the time", but it varies to its own rhythm, which does not track FANCA's — so no connection. "Both essential" is NOT "connected"; what is required is "varying together". (Hub genes like the ribosome, which are equally essential everywhere and barely vary at all, are a nastier trap — see step 6.)

STEP 4

Trap 1: screens from different contexts cannot be pooled

The problem. A plain proliferation screen and a cisplatin (DNA-damaging) screen measure completely different biology. Pooling every screen into one correlation does two bad things: (1) relationships that exist only in one context get diluted away; (2) genes that are essential under every condition (ribosome and friends) drop everywhere and therefore pretend to correlate with everyone.
The fix: make context a coordinate of the connection. Every screen is labelled first (build_net_context.py): proliferation, or some particular stress; and if it is a drug screen, it is placed in a mechanism-of-action bucket (drug_mechanism.py: cisplatin, etoposide, camptothecin ... all land in the "DNA / genotoxic" bucket). Correlation is then computed only within one context.

So the same pair of genes can carry different edges in different contexts — "A and B correlate under DNA damage" is a fact in its own right. That is the fundamental difference from STRING: STRING hands you one static line; we hand you a line that carries its context.

STEP 5

Trap 2: which genes are allowed to be nodes

The problem. Only about 7% of genes in a screen actually respond (the hits); the other 93% sit in the middle as noise. A gene that is never a hit has a fingerprint that is essentially random jitter — we have no evidence about it, and that unstable fingerprint will strike up spurious correlations that pollute the whole graph.
The fix: only genes carrying signal become nodes. To enter a context's network, a gene must have been a hit at least 5 times in that context. This cut removes roughly half the genes — mostly lincRNAs and uncharacterised "orphans" — and what remains is what actually does something in this context.

It looks trivial, but we learned it the hard way: an early version admitted every gene as a node, and the "top partners" of ribosomal genes came back as a pile of never-studied orphans — their fingerprints were so unstable that the maths amplified them into apparent similarity. Keeping the signal-free genes out cleaned the graph up immediately.

STEP 6

Trap 3: hub genes look like everybody

The problem. Some genes (ribosome, proteasome) are required simply for the cell to be alive. In proliferation screens they drop everywhere, and so return moderate correlations with a whole crowd of unrelated essential genes — a ribosomal gene "connects" to the proteasome, the spliceosome, and the network smears into a blob.
The fix: reciprocal rank (mutual best). For each gene, take its top-25 most correlated partners. Then an edge is marked reciprocal — the clean view shown by default — only when you are in my top-25 AND I am in yours.

Why does that work? A hub points at many genes, but each of those genes has its own complex members as ITS best partners — the hub does not make their list. So a hub's indiscriminate connections are not mutual, and drop out automatically. A genuine functional pair (FANCA ↔ FANCB) is each other's top choice in both directions, and survives. No external knowledge is consulted at any point — only the data's own ranking.

STEP 7

Finally: which edges actually get stored

Putting the rules together, for each gene, in each context:

· use genome-wide (FULL) screens only — a screen that reported only its hits cannot support a correlation
· require r ≥ 0.15 and ≥ 20 screens measuring both genes (support, so a high correlation cannot be a fluke of small numbers)
· keep each gene's top-25 partners
· store into net_edge: (gene A, gene B, context, strength, support, reciprocal)

Note the last line: one pair of genes can carry several edges, each hanging off a different context. That is what makes this a multi-edge, context-resolved network — and why switching context on the site rearranges the graph entirely.

STEP 8

How do we know any of this is right?

Computing something is not enough — the lines have to be shown to correspond to real biology. We use CORUM as the answer key: a hand-curated catalogue of protein complexes (which genes physically assemble and work together), 2,626 of them.

The test. Pairs from the same complex should correlate systematically higher than pairs from different complexes. Scored with AUROC: 0.5 = coin flip, 1.0 = perfect.

The prettier result is the context-specificity shot, which you can verify on the site yourself: the same FANCD2 has 4 mutual partners in the plain proliferation context; switch to the DNA-damage context and it has 19 — and they are the complete Fanconi / DNA-repair module (FANCA, FANCB, FANCC, FANCE, FANCI, RAD9A, RAD17, XRCC2, ESCO1, HELQ ...). Whether a pair connects, and what it connects into, differs by context. That is not a coincidence — it is the entire reason this algorithm exists: relationships are contextual, and you only see them clearly once the context is split correctly.

🧭 Tried, disproved by measurement, abandoned

Every "cleverer" idea was actually measured. None of them passed, which is why the final algorithm is the simple one — these choices were earned, not guessed.

✗
Hit-anchoring (correlate only over screens where a gene is a hit) — the professor's intuition, and it sounds right. Measured, AUROC fell by 0.09: hits are sparse, so most anchor sets end up "signal on one side, nothing on the other", which dilutes the real signal rather than concentrating it; and core essential genes get pinned at the extreme, lose their variance, and their correlations collapse into noise.
✗
CLR / principal-component denoising (the "sophisticated" hub controls) — falls apart genome-wide: it pours edges into orphan genes with very low background and pushes the genuine complex partners out. Plain reciprocal rank is the one that works.
✗
Validating against STRING — STRING is itself built from literature and co-occurrence. Using a literature network to validate a pure-CRISPR network contaminates the validation loop. So the answer key is CORUM complexes only — biochemical fact.
